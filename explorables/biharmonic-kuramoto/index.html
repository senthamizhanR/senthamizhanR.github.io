Each oscillator responds to the mean field in proportion to its own speed , through a first harmonic and a second harmonic . That is enough to split a single-peaked population into two or four frequency-sorted clusters. Pick a state, or move through the plane and watch the clusters form and break.

The model

What is being simulated

The frequency weighting makes fast oscillators respond more strongly to the mean field. The second harmonic has period π, so it can lock oscillators half a cycle apart. The two order parameters capture the difference: measures ordinary coherence, and detects π-periodic clustering, which can be close to 1 even when is zero.

The incoherent state loses linear stability at K₁ = 4 or K₂ = 4 (dashed lines on the plane), and the two harmonics decouple at linear order. In the antipodal state the cluster amplitude follows . The browser uses fourth-order Runge–Kutta with a step set by the fastest rate in the system. Natural frequencies sit on a Lorentzian quantile grid with the outer 1% of each tail left out (|ω| ≤ 31.8), so the stiffest oscillators stay tractable. The paper's runs use N = 2000 and an adaptive integrator.

The two coupling axes on their own were studied before: K₂ = 0 by Hu et al. (Sci. Rep. 2014) and K₁ = 0 by Tang, Lü & Xu (J. Phys. A 2021). The paper maps the interior of the plane, where the two harmonics compete.
